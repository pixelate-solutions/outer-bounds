Research synthesis & business plan · prepared October 7, 2026

Outer Bounds: a TrackMan-class launch monitor with no stickers and no subscription

Nine parallel research reports covered budget, mid and premium competitors, camera and radar engineering, open-source and academic work, patents and certification, the market, and manufacturing. This page turns them into one recommendation for what to build, what to charge and how to get there.

Technology
Cameras do the measuring. A small radar helps.

Indoors a driver turns only 1.4–2.4 times before it hits a screen 8–12 ft away. That is too little for radar to read spin off an unmarked ball, and radar never sees the club face. High-speed global-shutter cameras with microsecond infrared flashes measure spin, face, loft and impact on any ball. A ~$25–60 radar can add a pre-impact wake/trigger and an independent speed readout, kept separate from camera tracking to stay clear of TrackMan and Rapsodo patents (v1 can ship camera-only).

Form factor
Portable unit beside the ball, set slightly forward and angled back

It works in 10–12 ft rooms and on the range, is good at short game, and needs no ball-flight space behind the golfer. Setting it forward of the ball and yawing the cameras back 15–30° lets them see the club face rather than its toe. Two cameras are stacked vertically and a third is offset to the side to see the club.

Price
Pro $2,999 (founders $2,499) · Indoor $1,999 later

Three-year cost with GSPro is $3,749. The same period costs $5,547 for a Bushnell Launch Pro with Gold and GSPro, $5,750 for a Garmin R50 and $9,546 for a Foresight GC3. The price sits above Square Omni ($1,599, needs stickers) and below the $5k R50 and Full Swing KIT.

Business model
No subscription, ever. Every metric unlocked. Open API.

GSPro, OpenGolfSim and an open shot protocol from day one, with a published local API. Revenue comes from hardware margin (about 62–72% gross margin direct at $2,999), plus bundles, accessories, a one-time Coach/Fitter license and B2B range kits.

The moat (and biggest risk)
Measured, markerless face angle, dynamic loft and impact location from a portable unit

No portable unit at any price under ~$13k does this today. GC3, Bushnell, R50, Uneekor and Square all use stickers. It is the hardest engineering problem in the plan, so it is gated: honest labelling of measured vs derived values, plus go/no-go accuracy checks against a GCQuad.

Timeline & capital
Ship holiday 2028 (stretch) / spring 2029 (realistic). About $12–17M to launch.

A $4–6M seed round carries us to the first engineering build with accuracy proven. A $10–15M Series A at design validation pays for production inventory and launch. Final assembly goes in Taiwan or Vietnam, not China: US duty is about 10–14% there against about 39% for China.

Why this is the best value position Below ~$1,600 you get speed and launch measurements and modelled everything else. Club data is calculated, behind a paywall or needs stickers, and spin needs special balls. From $2,999 to $7,000 incumbents sell sticker-based club data and recover margin through $199–$599/year subscriptions. Foresight raised Gold to $599 on Sept 18, 2026. We charge Bushnell Launch Pro money once, deliver more club data than a $7k GC3 without stickers, and let the owner use any simulator. The 3-year cost comparison is the main pitch.

Section 6

Radar vs camera vs hybrid: the best value for money

The decision follows from physics. It is not a matter of preference.

Why radar alone fails our brief

Indoors the ball is visible for only ~35–110 ms before the screen. A 2,600 rpm drive turns 1.4–2.4 times in that window, so the frequency resolution (19–30 Hz) is about the same as the spin itself (43 Hz). TrackMan's own patent says an unmarked ball's spin signal "is often too weak to be useful" indoors. That is why Mevo needs metal dots, R10 calculates spin, and TrackMan's indoor iO measures spin with a camera. Radar also never sees which way the face points, so face angle and loft are back-calculated and impact location is impossible.

Why cameras win, if exposures are gated

At 940 nm with a 5 µs exposure timed to the flash, full sunlight adds only ~4% to the signal. With the cheap trick of one 1 ms open shutter and several flashes (SkyTrak, PiTrac), sunlight is ~8× the signal and the image fails. That is why those units struggle or fail outdoors. 940 nm has under half the solar energy of 850 nm. Microsecond flashes also freeze a 200 mph ball to under 0.5 mm of blur, which is sharp enough to read dimples for spin.

Why add a small radar anyway

For ~$25–60 in parts, a 24 GHz radar with 1 transmitter and 3–4 receivers gives: (1) a trigger from the approaching club tens of ms before impact, so the cameras capture the club too; (2) an independent ball and club speed check that catches camera calibration drift; (3) outdoors, 30–60 m of measured flight, so carry is anchored to data rather than a model alone. TrackMan, FlightScope and Rapsodo have all converged on radar plus camera. Patent catch: combining radar data with camera tracks is patented by TrackMan (to 2037–41) and Rapsodo (to 2039). Our radar therefore stays a separate sensor in v1 (wake + speed readout). Outdoor flight tracking and any real fusion wait for the FTO opinion, and v1 can ship camera-only.

The camera choices you asked about: how many, and inline vs staggered

Number of cameras

  • 1 camera + flashes (SkyTrak, PiTrac): cheapest, but depth comes from apparent ball size (horizontal launch ±0.6°) and it is indoor-only.
  • 2 cameras (stereo pair) (EYE MINI, GC2): good 3D ball position and spin. Club pose is weak in some directions.
  • 3 cameras (GC3, Launch Pro, R50): two baselines give robust club edges (shaft, leading edge, top line) at every angle, plus redundancy against occlusion. This is our pick: a vertical stereo pair plus one offset club camera.
  • 4 cameras (GCQuad, Square Omni): two stacked pairs. Mostly buys a bigger hitting zone and time diversity. Uneekor gets full marker-based club data from 2.

Inline vs vertical vs staggered

  • Inline (horizontal baseline): the ball travels along the matching lines between the two images, so several ball images on one line become ambiguous. The left and right edges it measures are also the ones motion blur smears.
  • Vertical (stacked) baseline: matches run perpendicular to travel, so every ball image is unique and the top and bottom edges stay sharp. The unit stays narrow and tower-shaped. Square Omni and GCQuad stack vertically, and so do we, with a 60–100 mm baseline.
  • Staggered / triangular: adding a horizontally offset third camera gives a second baseline for club pose. Cameras can also fire at different instants for more time samples.
  • Capture method: high-fps video is a big data pipe. One long exposure with several flashes fails in sun. Gated quad-shutter (Sony IMX900: 4 separately timed sub-images per frame) gives multi-image spin in full sun on a mid-price sensor.

Section 8

Markerless club data: the hard problem, and our approach

In October 2026 no camera launch monitor sold beside the ball measures face angle or impact location without stickers. Foresight still needs 4 dots even on its $17k Falcon. Uneekor's sticker-free EYE XR calculates face angle from the ball. TrackMan reports club data on "over 90%" of shots. This is why the opportunity exists, and it plays to a software-strong team.

How we get it

  1. Geometry first. The forward-oblique placement plus a vertical pair and an offset camera means the face is actually visible, rather than seen edge-on as from a straight side view.
  2. Capture timing. A rolling buffer at a constant capture mode (optionally woken by the radar) keeps 2–3 club images within ~0.5 ms of impact. A driver face closes at roughly 1,500–3,000°/s, so timing matters more than resolution.
  3. Multi-view face-plane fit. A network trained on synthetic renders of hundreds of club heads finds heel, toe, top line, leading edge and iron grooves. Grooves are free natural markers. We fit the whole face surface across all three views and take face, loft and lie from the face normal. We never take them from a single reference point or a shaft "fix line": that is TrackMan's patented single-camera approach (to ~2039). Published work suggests under 1° is achievable.
  4. Physics prior. Ball launch direction, spin axis and spin loft constrain face and loft (the D-plane). That catches gross errors and gives a confidence score.
  5. Optional "show your club" scan. A one-time 10-second scan of each club at address builds an exact model.

How we keep it honest and on schedule

  • Data engine: record every bay shot side by side with a GCQuad wearing its 4-dot stickers as the reference while our unit ignores them, plus marked-ball sessions and synthetic data. Targets: 50k labelled shots by the first engineering build (EVT) and 200k by design validation (DVT).
  • Measured vs derived, shown in the UI. If a shot's face confidence is low, we show the physics-derived value labelled as derived, not a guess presented as a measurement. TrackMan's own >90% pickup rate shows a fallback is needed.
  • Go/no-go gate G2: path and face within 1° (95th percentile) on all clubs before tooling. If we miss it, launch with face and loft labelled "derived" and ship the measured version as a free firmware update. Never add stickers.
  • Validation that sells: publish a white paper using the Leach et al. (2017) method (bias and limits of agreement per club against TrackMan/GCQuad). No independent test of markerless face data exists for any product today.
Section 9

Software & open ecosystem

Software is where a software-strong team can lead, and where the category is weakest. Reviews describe the R10 app as "in desperate need of an overhaul", Uneekor Refine as "widely criticized" and Square's software as "bare bones".

Simulator connections

  • GSPro Open Connect, day one: public, TCP 127.0.0.1:921, JSON with no framing, no auth. Users buy GSPro's "OpenAPI" licence ($250/yr). Test against libgspro's 115 cases (MIT).
  • Native GSPro listing: business@gsprogolf.com. Small makers (OpenLaunch Nova, Bluetees) have been listed.
  • OpenGolfSim (TCP 3111) and Flight Relay Protocol (an open, public-domain launch-monitor data standard). We could be the first hardware vendor to adopt FRP.
  • E6 / TruGolf (TruSimAPI port 2483) needs a business deal, because its authentication appears to be under NDA. Awesome Golf is partner-only.

Our own apps (free)

  • iPad / iPhone / Android / Windows practice app: driving range, dispersion, gapping, combines, session history, video replay of impact, exports.

Ball-flight model & licence rules

  • Write our own: drag/lift curves from Ferguson et al. 2022 (fitted on 1,040 shots; ~2.9 yd carry error vs TrackMan in open tests), a correction for slow shots, spin decay, and the Penner bounce + roll model. Tune per surface against TrackMan totals.
  • Do not copy PiTrac (GPL-2.0; its AI models are non-commercial) or OpenFlight (AGPL-3.0). Learn from them and implement clean-room. Equations and published coefficients are free to use.
  • Safe to use: libgspro (MIT), Flight Relay Protocol SDK (Apache/MIT), SpinDOE (Apache-2.0), ED_LIB ellipse fitting (MIT).

Section 13

Patents, litigation, certification & the name

The core techniques are now free to use. The risk is in a set of newer, narrower patents, mostly from TrackMan and Rapsodo, that cover the exact features that set us apart. This is groundwork, not legal advice. A freedom-to-operate (FTO) opinion from a patent attorney (~$40–120k for a focused one) is required before tooling.

Good news: the base is free

The original markerless camera-spin patents from Wintriss Engineering, later owned by Foresight, expired in 2025: US 7,292,711 in April and US 7,324,663 in August. They cover an unmarked ball and spin found by rotating and correlating its dimples, scuffs and logo. The camera triggers itself, and an accelerometer handles levelling. Acushnet's 1990s stereo and flash patents and the 1990s radar Doppler-speed patents have also expired. Our core spin method can rest on this free prior art.

Main risk: markerless club data

TrackMan US 10,953,303 / 11,439,886 / 11,612,801 / 12,263,393 (run to ~2038–39) cover markerless single-camera impact location, club direction from rate-of-distance change, and dynamic loft or lie from a shaft "fix line" angle. Our design-around is multi-view stereo fitting of the whole face plane, with loft and lie taken from the face normal, never from the shaft. Counsel must chart this.

Radar is the most crowded layer

TrackMan holds patents on radar spin (to 2029), spin axis from 3 or more receivers (to ~2039), combining radar with camera tracks (to 2037) and calibrating radar against cameras (to 2041). Rapsodo holds a patent on radar tracking that triggers a camera and combines the data (to 2039). Full Swing covers mixing two radar modes (CW and FMCW, to 2039). So radar stays a separate wake and speed sensor whose data never feeds into the camera's position estimate. Shipping v1 with no radar at all is the lowest-risk option.

Litigation: who actually sues

  • Patent trolls (NPEs) come first. Wyoming IP Holdings sued TrackMan over a generic "sensor + coaching" patent and lost with prejudice in 2024: the court ruled the patent was an abstract idea and invalid under §101. It then sued Bushnell over the Launch Pro, and Bushnell settled within about 4 months. Expect a demand letter once we have revenue. Budget ~$50–150k.
  • TrackMan v. GSPro (S.D.N.Y., still in discovery): copyright and false-advertising claims over simulator courses and code. Integrate only through documented APIs and never touch course assets.
  • No patent suits between the major brands were found in US federal records. The established makers hold large portfolios but haven't fought each other in court. A visible newcomer copying club-data or fusion features is the most likely trigger.
  • Our move: file provisional patents before any public demo (~$3–8k each), keep dated design-around records, and track TrackMan and Rapsodo continuations.

The name "Outer Bounds"

  • No live US registration for "OUTER BOUNDS" (four dead ones in classes 18, 24, 25 and 28).
  • Conflict: OUTERBOUNDS (Reg. 6,997,555, live) in classes 9, 35, 41 and 42, owned by the data-science/ML company Outerbounds (Metaflow, joining Anaconda). It also owns outerbounds.com and will dominate search results. Risk is moderate for app/software classes and low for hardware (class 28 / class 9 hardware).
  • Also live: OUTTA BOUNDS GOLF (driving range, class 41).
  • Recommendation: attorney clearance search (~$1.5–5k). File intent-to-use in classes 9 (hardware) and 28 now, or switch to a compound or coined name such as "Outer Bounds Golf", and clear back-up names in parallel.
